## My theory on grit, suppression, and the feedback that becomes chronic pain

Many of the people I work with are at once the toughest and somehow also the most sensitive people.

Some are mentally tough while physically frail, but for most those lines are less obvious.

These people are my people. They’re **neuroplastic people**.

They’re the ones who twisted themselves like a pretzel into whatever shape they needed to – to make the least waves, the least noise, take up the least space, and have the least needs.

Many grew up in homes with chronically stressed parents – whether from volatility in the market or because the local factory had layoffs again.

They often come from tough stock. People who worked hard and got the job done. No. Matter. What. Many neuroplastic people inherited the same work ethic.

These are people that when the stress comes, they press on. When the pain comes, they ignore it. When exhaustion and medical bills come, they get that fifth cup of coffee and go back to work.

**But that kind of grit has a price.**

Most people – including doctors, clinicians, and therapists – don’t know this, but there are **two filters in your brain**. One controls what you hear in your own monitor (think of it like an in‑ear earpiece). The other controls the mains – the speakers whose output is essentially fight or flight.

And if you use the first filter too much, the second one will eventually scream at you with pain, fatigue, and an overwhelming urge to quit.

## **Filter One: The Monitor Mute Button (RTN)**

Deep in the middle of your brain sits the **Reticular Thalamic Nucleus (RTN)**. Think of it as the mute button for your **monitor** – that’s the speaker on stage that lets you hear yourself. Your conscious awareness is the monitor.

Your prefrontal cortex (the conscious “you”) can activate this mute. When you decide “I don’t have time for this neck pain” or “I can't think about that grief I never had time to feel,” your PFC tells the RTN: **cut that channel from my monitor.**

And the RTN obeys. It releases GABA (inhibitory neuropeptide) onto the relay station that would normally send that signal to your conscious awareness. Poof. You don’t hear it anymore. You can “focus on what matters.”

But here’s the thing that no one tells you. **You only muted the monitor. The mains are still live and you *can’t* mute those.**

## **Filter Two: The Mains Volume (RAS)**

The signal from your neck – or the emotional charge from that unfelt grief – doesn’t just vanish. It travels up a **parallel pathway** that bypasses the RTN entirely. It goes straight to your brain stem, where a different filter lives.

That filter is the **Reticular Activating System (RAS)** . The RAS is the **master volume for the mains** – the speakers pointed at the audience. In your body, the audience is your autonomic nervous system, and the mains are your fight‑or‑flight response.

* **RAS low** = mains quiet (rest and digest, calm, safe) Parasympathetic 
* **RAS high** = mains blasting (heart beats faster, muscles tighten, on edge) sympathetic 

When your brain stem keeps receiving those ignored signals – day after day, year after year – it interprets them as a **persistent unresolved threat**. So it cranks the RAS to **HIGH**. It cranks your nervous system toward sympathetic activation, fight of flight. 

**You are now living with the mains at full volume** – even though there’s no tiger, no emergency, nothing you can point to. You just feel … loud. Inside. All the time. Some of us even learn to love the loud.

## **The Feedback Loop: Hot wires - muted monitors - and live mains**

Now here’s where neuroplastic pain comes from. You already know what happens when you mute a channel in your monitor (your conscious awareness) you can't hear it anymore so you assume everything is fine. But the signal is still live, loud, and pointed at the crowd, your nervous systems RAS, it’s fight or flight mechanism. 

So you think everything should be fine. The signal isn't coming through to your awareness so it can’t be you. Something else has got to be something broken in the system. Then you say things like “I’ve been dealing with this for years.” “That can't be it, it's hardly even a thought.” “barely a blip”

That’s exactly what happens in your brain. You mute the neck pain in your monitor, so you stop feeling it. But your neck still hurts. The signal from your neck is still hitting your brain stem, and your brain stem is cranking the mains – your fight‑or‑flight system – because it senses something is wrong. 

Then you mute the back pain, then the eviction notice, then the rent increase, then climate change, then ICE, Then you remember that your youngest daughter is now dating age and you are definitely not ready for that, let alone for that possible promotion or the new HOA rules or your boss’s shit again……. Mute. Mute. Mute. Mute and mute some more. You see where I’m going?

You mute each and every one of those channels but ONLY in *your* monitor, your *conscious awareness,* day after day, week after week year after year. Your heart beats faster. Your muscles stay tight. You’re on edge but you don’t know why. And that becomes your normal.

Then one day you turn your head a certain way. Or something small reminds you of that old grief – a song, a smell, a date on the calendar. Or you sit down to finally *rest*. And suddenly – *feedback*. A migraine. A wave of vertigo. A back spasm that drops you to the floor. Exhaustion so heavy, you can’t lift your eyelids.

You think: *“What just happened? I was fine a second ago.”*

But you weren’t fine. Monitor muted incoming stress signals have been blasting your central nervous system into high alert *for years.* It’s compromised your immune system, your nervous system, your cardiovascular system for decades. You were just able to ignore most of it because you muted it in your monitor.

The feedback is not the problem. The signal is not the problem. The feedback is the evidence that the mains have been live and cranked the whole time. And every time you get close to a muted source – a neck position, a memory, a person – the system howls.

*That howl is neuroplastic pain.* It can look like anything. *Migraine. IBS. Chronic fatigue. Fibromyalgia. Back pain. Vertigo. Tinnitus. Panic.* They all share this same mechanism: a cranked mains, a muted monitor, and proximity to a source you thought you’d turned off.

## **The Variety Among Neuroplastic Symptoms** 

Now let me tell you about Bruce McEwen, because his work is foundational as to why any symptom can be a neuroplastic symptom. McEwen spent decades at Rockefeller University asking a simple question: 

### *What happens to the body when stress **doesn’t** go away?*

All that stress you mute. The stress you shunt. The stress you ignore because you have too much to do. You *ain't got time for that* and you’re not going to be a problem.

He called it **allostatic load**. It’s the wear and tear on your body from chronic, unresolved stress. And here’s what he found happens when the RAS stays cranked year after year.

Your *immune system* starts acting like it’s always under attack. Inflammation goes up. Cytokines (inflammation markers) flood your blood. You get labeled with “autoimmune.” 

Your *endocrine system* loses its rhythm. Cortisol stops spiking in the morning and dropping at night. Sometimes it’s high all the time, sometimes it’s flat, but either way your HPA axis is burned out. You wake up exhausted and you can’t fall asleep.

Your *cardiovascular system* takes a hit too. Blood pressure stays elevated. Resting heart rate stays high. Your vessels stiffen over time. That’s why chronic stress is a risk factor for heart disease – not because of the stress itself, but because the stress never goes away. The mains never turn off and the body has to adapt. 

Your *metabolism* gets weird. Insulin resistance creeps in. You gain weight around your middle that won’t budge no matter how much you exercise. Even mitochondria get overworked to the point they break down and stop producing as much energy. This is how chronic stress adapted people become easily fatigued. It’s a metabolic conservation strategy to combat decades of living in fight or flight. 

Even your *brain* changes. Chronic high allostatic load shrinks your prefrontal cortex – the CEO that makes the reasonable choice to ignore things – and shrinks your amygdala, the fear center. So you get worse at muting strategically and better at feeling threatened by everything. 

*The loop feeds itself.*

***This is* why *any symptom can be a neuroplastic symptom.*** *Because the mains – the fight‑or‑flight system – connect to every organ, every gland, every immune cell.* 

When the mains stay cranked, the feedback can show up as a migraine in one person, IBS in another, chronic fatigue in a third. The symptom is not the disease. The symptom is the feedback. The mismatch between the cognitive and physiological load. The dis-ease is the incredible, unresolved allostatic stress load created by decades of muting your monitor!

McEwen proved that ignored stress doesn’t stay in your brain or just go away. It seeps into every corner of your biology. That’s not a metaphor. That’s a fact. And it’s why neuroplastic people get sick in a hundred different ways from the same underlying loop.

***Next let's talk about why you can't make yourself do all the crap you know you “should” do.***

## **The Quit Switch (it’s a biological one)**

When the mains stay cranked for too long, your brain stem releases a neuropeptide called **nociceptin**. Nociceptin is associated with the experience of pain. Its long tendril-like shape means upon release (pain) it mops up dopamine – your motivation chemical. It’s your brain’s **biological off‑switch for effort**.

It turns out we don’t decide to quit. The switch  flips. Then your cortex invents a reason: “I don’t care anymore” or “This isn’t worth it.”

That reason comes **after** the quit – not before.

Neuroplastic people don’t quit easily. But when they do, it's their body that gives out. And it gives out *hard.* 

More often than not the neuroplastic person's conscious self is on board with doing the thing, while their body says “absolutely not.” Then slaps them in the face with whichever symptom is mostly likely to be the most debilitating enough to make them give up.  

## **What This Means for Clinicians (and for the tough people who end up in your office)**

I need you to hear something. When a patient like this walks in – the one who is tough, whose case is tough, and who's tough to deal with, you should not ask them to be *tougher.*

What they have is a nervous system that learned a survival strategy: mute the monitor, ignore the problem, keep going, don’t be a problem. That strategy saved them once. Now it’s killing them. 

Because they have successfully muted many of their channels with lots of success for a long time it often takes time and convincing, and usually their own explorations are the only thing that will convince them *of* the source. 

And because the mains connect to every system – *immune, endocrine, cardiovascular* – that feedback can land literally anywhere. 

That’s why the same mechanism (muting stress) causes migraines in one person, IBS in another, chronic fatigue in a third. The symptom is the body’s vocabulary for “I got close to a muted stress source”

What they need is to unmute the monitor. One channel at a time. Because each one is gonna be blaring. Over time, teach them to notice what they’ve been muting. 

The neck tension they’ve been ignoring. The grief they never had time to feel. The childhood they had to survive. The job that asked them to disappear. The relationship they held together by sheer will.

The first step is to **turn down the mains**. A person with Loud Main speakers is a person in the middle of a fight of flight response and doesn't know it. They are in a threat response mode. Which means convincing their neurology they are safe. 

Unfortunately people have very little control over their Autonomic Nervous System and you as a clinician can’t reach inside a patient to turn down some magical Fight or Flight dimmer knob. So what do you do?

*Breathing* is my favorite way to have people downregulate (turn down the mains). Specifically the *physiological sigh* is the best for quick reregulation. 

***A physiological sigh*** is a rapid, two-step breathing technique—double inhale followed by a long, slow exhale—designed to rapidly reduce stress and activate the parasympathetic nervous system. 

A few rounds of physiological sighs and any patient will feel more regulated and more safe. You will know a person is safe enough to unmute a channel when their curiosity reappears. From there it's an association game triangulating feedback symptomology with proximity to muted channels.

Pro tip: Look around what they're best at, enjoy the most and identify the most with. The earliest and deepest adaptations often get tied into our personalities. Which means after you look at what they're best at, look at the things they have an absolute aversion to or hatred for. The thing they would rather die than do or be associated with. Chances are there’s a mute button or ten near there.

## **The Bottom Line**

Here’s what I believe after watching this play out hundreds of times.

Neuroplastic people don’t need to get tougher. They need to get **more specific**. They need to stop twisting into shapes that don’t fit and start noticing what they’ve been muting. The pain is not the enemy. The pain is feedback and it only happens when we have come close to a stress source. Which makes pain and other unexplainable neuroplastic symptoms an orientation stimulus. Meaning, when the symptoms come don't avoid dismiss or ignore them. Look closer. 

Every time you mute a signal – whether it’s the neck pain you ignore to keep working, or the unavoidable reality that someone important will never be who you needed them to be (looking at you Dad)– you crank the fight‑or‑flight system. 

A cranked fight‑or‑flight means allostatic load: wear and tear on every system in your body. That’s why the same mechanism can cause a thousand different symptoms. The symptom is not the disease. The symptom is the feedback. The disease is the allostatic load.

You are not lazy. You are not weak. You are not making it up.

You are a person who learned to survive by turning off your own monitor. And now your central nervous system is screaming because the only way it knows to get your attention is to create feedback loud enough that you can’t ignore it.

The way out is not more coffee. It’s not more grit. And it’s certainly not another diagnosis.

It’s noticing the feedback and asking: What did I mute? Was it the pain so I could make a paycheck? Was it the pain of the truth that they will never be who I need them to be? Or maybe from admitting what *actually* happened to you and how much it hurt?

And then, very slowly, unmuting each channel – not to wallow, but to finally feel the emotional reality that has been consciously ignored and therefore somatically processed. turn down the alarm and lower your allostatic load.

That’s not a TED talk slogan. That’s the hardest thing you’ll ever do. But it’s the only thing that i know works.
